Meta AI director Rohit Patel leaves to take on Microsoft Office and Google Workspace

Patel designed Ndoc to be more than just another AI tool layered onto an existing workflow. His desire to open-source this project was partly why he left Meta and decided to go it alone. “Companies are basically attaching AI to spreadsheets and it fills your cells for you,” he said.

Key details from the source

Editorial digest: additional context drawn from the original reporting. Open the primary source for quotes, figures, and updates.

After eight years developing AI for Meta, Rohit Patel is departing the company, TechCrunch has exclusively learned. The former director at Meta Superintelligence Labs has launched Ndoc , a startup he’s pitching as an AI-native replacement for Microsoft Office suite or Google Workspace. Patel is the latest in a handful of high-profile departures from Meta following the exits of Chief Global Affairs Officer Joel Kaplan , AI researcher Andrew Tulloch , and Head of Product for AI-for-Work Emily Dalton Smith .

The goal is to replace the fragmented way people work across tools like Excel, Word, PowerPoint, Tableau, Replit, and “ChatGPT copy-paste” with one unified workspace that all colleagues can access, even if they’re not signed up for Ndoc. Ndoc brings three components together in one layout. The Stage tab is where presentations of key findings live, like slide decks, dashboards, simulations, or mini apps.

The Narrative tab features analyses, numbered citations, generated figures, and generally the “why” behind the numbers. The Model tab is where underlying datasets and Python scripts are housed. During his tenure at Meta, Patel focused on reinforcement learning — a type of machine learning in which AI agents learn to make decisions through trial and error and maximize rewards — and evaluating whether AI output is trustworthy.

Patel believes that if AI is going to build the spreadsheets and slide decks that companies use to make decisions, someone should be able to check its work. For instance, users can find the Python scripts used to create the documents. “Even if you don’t know any Python, you can ask [the AI chat] to explain, ‘Hey, what does this script do?

Which line has my formula?’ And you can verify that formula,” Patel said. “There’s nothing hidden behind the curtain here.” In practice, users can upload a dataset and ask Ndoc to perform an analysis and write a narrative about the findings. They can also add document-specific context, including audience, tone, and memory.

“It maintains a memory across your person, that particular document, your team, and your company,” Patel said. Patel also says Ndoc is built and fine-tuned using a mixture of open source models. Because the platform was developed from scratch, he says it costs considerably less to run than alternatives built on frontier AI models.

“I thought that’s what people would be excited about, but people don’t care about that,” he said, referring to his early trial users. “What people are excited about is that they love seeing all their end-to-end in one place.” Patel’s larger ambition is to reinvent the file itself. He says documents can be exported as a single “.ndoc.html” file that can be opened in any browser, a format he plans to open-source so other developers can build apps that work with it without being locked into Ndoc the way so many have been locked into…

At a big company like Meta, getting everyone to agree on an open source format would undoubtedly require the input of “hundreds of people and opinions on how the format should look.” Patel wanted to move quickly. “Humans can only move at human pace, whereas…I can move at the pace of what AI can do.” Trying to become the platform where enterprise work happens is a huge bet for a founder with a team of three and no funding, especially when other AI labs are trying to own the same space. OpenAI, Anthropic , Meta , SpaceXAI , and Google…

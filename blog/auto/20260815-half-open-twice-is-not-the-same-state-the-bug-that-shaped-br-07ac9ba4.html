"half-open" twice is not the same state: the bug that shaped breakwater 1.0

breakwater is my resilience toolkit for Node.js — retry, circuit breaker, timeout, bulkhead, rate limiting, stale-while-open caching, all composable, with observability built in. It just hit 1.0.0 , and the headline feature is the one no Node library did well: a circuit breaker whose state is shared across every instance of your service . The others fail fast immediately, without each having to discover the same outage on their own users.

This post is about the two things I got wrong on the way there, because they were both the kind of wrong that looks right. Part 1: a state machine where the same name means two different things A circuit breaker is a tiny state machine. In one process you protect transitions with nothing at all — JavaScript is single-threaded, and the code between two await s cannot be interrupted.

So the store interface had: Atomic in Redis via a Lua script. That decision travels — a Lua round trip to Redis, a few milliseconds. In those milliseconds, another probe succeeds , reaches the majority, and closes the circuit.

The circuit reopens, waits out the cooldown, and enters half-open again . And it is completely wrong — that decision belonged to a period that ended three transitions ago, and it just killed a recovery that had nothing to do with it. This is the ABA problem , and it is easy to miss here because the states have names .

It is not: it is a label that the circuit wears repeatedly, and comparing labels tells you nothing about whether you are still in the world you made your decision in. My first fix was a patch, and I knew it I noticed the race before I had a distributed store — the in-process breaker had the same window whenever a custom store was async. So I patched it: after the swap succeeded, check whether the period had flipped, and if it had, swap the state back .

I shipped it because the alternative was redesigning the store contract, and I wasn't ready. The stale decision from step 4 now carries fence 7 against a store holding fence 10. Redis refuses it, atomically, in the same script.

Both compensating transitions were deleted . The code got shorter, which is how you know the design got better. Two details that fell out of it, and that I would not have thought to add: The failed swap returns where the circuit actually is.

Losing a race used to cost a second round trip to find out what happened. Now the outcome carries the current snapshot — you lose the race and refresh your view in one shot. openedAt lives in Redis, stamped from the server clock.

Why this matters

It says: "if the state is still half-open , make it open ." The state is half-open . It reframes risk for teams that automate sensitive workflows and rely on third-party data flows. It highlights where brittle integrations become operational debt as vendors iterate quickly.

Operational takeaways

  • The circuit reopens, waits out the cooldown, and enters half-open again .
  • And it is completely wrong — that decision belonged to a period that ended three transitions ago, and it just killed a recovery that had nothing to do with it.
  • Two swaps are not one swap: between them, another instance sees the wrong state, and the compensating swap can itself fail.

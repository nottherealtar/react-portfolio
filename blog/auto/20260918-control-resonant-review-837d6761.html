Control Resonant review

Melee action in an open world being ripped apart by interdimensional forces. Resonant's handful of boss fights are more engaging threats to navigate (though the sheer heft of their health bars still feels like a slog).

Key details from the source

Editorial digest: additional context drawn from the original reporting. Open the primary source for quotes, figures, and updates.

In the first minutes of Control Resonant, Dylan Faden—prototypical sadboy, perpetually kicked puppy, and regretful mass murderer (he got better)—is thrust into a metaphysical war when Jesse, his psychokinetic former protagonist sister, wakes him from a seven year coma by stabbing a magical shape into his chest. She promptly disappears, leaving the Federal Bureau of Control directorless, outgunned, and forced to rely on the man who once killed hundreds of its staff to confront the cataclysm of interdimensional forces violently…

Unfortunately, melee combat isn't on that list of strong suits—and Resonant's open world expects you to do a lot of it. Remedy's environmental artists and lighting tech specialists are at the height of their powers in Resonant: The stark, brutalist surrealism that saturated the Oldest House in the first game has boiled out of its breached walls and onto New York's streets, reconstructing blocks of brownstones and downtown shopping districts into a sprawling, dreamlike mazework of impossible architecture and alternative physics.…

Rows of pixel-stretched structures bleed into chasms where chunks of cityscape have simply fallen away. Whole neighborhoods spiral upwards, outwards, inwards. Some are swallowed in the telltale oilslick haze of Hiss corruption.

Others have been reconfigured according to the alien logic of the Patterning—a newer, stranger force whose spreading influence sorts apartments, taxis, streetsigns, pigeons, and unevacuated denizens into inscrutable designs of sprawling radial symmetry. It's arresting, at turns breathtakingly incomprehensible and queasily familiar. Nestled into the mutated Manhattan's warped streets are pockets of untouched city life: abandoned cafes and laundromats and bespoke art galleries, which only heighten the sense of eeriness through their…

It's gorgeous, a sequel that's begging for an inevitable photo mode update. And it's a pleasure to move through, with Dylan's accumulating collection of mobility superpowers—floating, air-dashing, double-jumping, and eventually, freely reorienting gravity—allowing for truly impressive feats of traversal design. I just wish I could say the same for all the hours of fighting I had to do.

While Jesse wielded a manifold pseudogun in the OG Control, Dylan employs a different sort of shape-shifting weapon technique courtesy of the object his sister impaled in his sternum. It's called the Aberrant, and in his hands, it can assume and discard the shapes of different melee weapons on demand: Depending on your preference, it can be a hatchet; a scythe, a pair of short swords, a hammer, a drill, an orbiting, levitating sawblade, and more. Playing a guy who can throw rocks with his brain and has a stick that can be infinite…

While eventual unlocks add necessary depth and variety, Resonant's opening hours suffer from the cardinal sin of its combat design: It's an action game where basic attacks don't have the juice. On its own, pushing the attack button won't achieve much except lightly grazing the heavy enemies that need to be killed to finish each fight. Instead, Dylan has to pile on temporary damage bonuses—granted most easily by staggering and executing lesser enemies—to make swinging his magical mystery club worthwhile.

But the endless rounds of generic creep culling that make up the bulk of playtime don't provide particularly interesting problems to solve. Instead, they're the same predicament with a binary outcome: Either you've stacked enough damage buffs that hitting a heavy enemy will accomplish something, or you can spend your time whittling away at its health bar until you get a chance to set yourself up properly. To be fair, sometimes a guy will have a forcefield on him.

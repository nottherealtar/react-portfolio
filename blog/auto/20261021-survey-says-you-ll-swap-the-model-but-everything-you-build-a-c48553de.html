Kubernetes has thrived over the past decade in part because enterprises want to pick their vendors, not belong to them. Does that sentiment persist as we move into the AI-native era? Earlier this month, my team at Stacklok surveyed 520 technical leaders to understand whether they were concerned about being locked into frontier labs.

The data says they’re already swapping models in and out, but the trap they’re concerned about is the layer on top of the model. Every respondent runs LLMs or agents in pilot or production at a North American company with more than 500 employees and holds a manager-or-above title. Only 8% of respondent organizations use one model provider, whereas 25% use five or more.

It’s already a multi-model world in the enterprise. Lock-in at the model layer appears to be a solved problem. Multi-step agent workflows are right behind at 52%.

Guardrails and access-control config come next. And if you move to another model provider, these integrations don’t move with you; they have to be re-built. More than 80% of respondent organizations have already delayed, scaled back, or restructured a migration because switching costs looked too high; 29% say it happens frequently.

And when asked how long a migration off their current provider would take, 57% said four months or more. But as someone who thinks a lot about preserving optionality and avoiding lock-in, here’s the part that stings. When asked what actually factors into AI, "Ability to switch model providers without rebuilding" ranked sixth out of eight possible criteria.

"Avoiding dependence on a single vendor" ranked dead last. The ranking is rational, and that’s what stings. Security and privacy are the priorities, followed by cost control and compliance.

The person choosing the stack today gets judged on this quarter’s bill and this year’s audit, not on a rebuild two years from now; migrations between models and providers is a problem for later (and possibly for a future team).

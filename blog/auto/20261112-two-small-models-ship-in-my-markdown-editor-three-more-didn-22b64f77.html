Two small models ship in my Markdown editor. Three more didn't.

Each model picks one answer from a fixed list, like which language a code block is in. It got 391 suggestions right at 93.8% precision, against 380 at 93.1% for the model already in the app. Marksage is a Markdown editor I built.

On October 30, I ran a new code-language model once against a test set it had never seen. This model existed to fix the near-twin languages: TypeScript and JavaScript, C++ and C, C# and Java. On exactly those languages, the new one lost: 103 right at 88.0%, against 106 at 89.8%.

Better on average, worse where it was aimed. A model that wins the scoreboard and misses its own goal isn't an upgrade. That decision is the whole article in miniature.

It has no backend, no accounts and no analytics: your documents stay in your browser. Its assistant, Smart Assist, suggests fixes as you write. Behind some of those suggestions are small models trained in the repository.

This is how they're built, how they're measured, and why most of what I trained isn't in the app. Every question it asks has its answers listed up front, and the engine has to pick from the list. The whole contract is three methods: choice picks an option.

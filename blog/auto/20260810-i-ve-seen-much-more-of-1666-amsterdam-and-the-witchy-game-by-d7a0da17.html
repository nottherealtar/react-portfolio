1666: Amsterdam returned to the public eye this year after a protracted 15 year conception that involved legal battles, rewritten concepts, secret publishers, and finally: an actual game. Suffice to say, this is the game Patrice Désilets has wanted to make for a very long time - Désilets being the original creator of Assassin's Creed and more recently the mind behind primate evolution game Ancestors: The Humankind Odyssey . But the return of 1666: Amsterdam - developed by Désilets' Panache Digital studio, which he co-founded in 2014…

It was a glimpse of 1666 Amsterdam that left me with as many questions as it did answers , such as 'What will the combat be like?', 'Will we be able to actually explore Amsterdam?', and 'These overlapping timelines are confusing.' Okay, that last one isn't a question, but it's true; its action takes place in 1666, 1999, and the present day, and moves between the three of them. There were a lot of ideas not especially well explained. During the trip, he takes us around the city to show us places modelled in-game.

He even shows us the inspirational "Anno 1666" plaque he saw on a wall while on a fateful aeroplane layover here in 2011, which ultimately inspired the game. And standing among these tall, tottering buildings, his idea to make an Assassin's Creed-like experience set in Amsterdam absolutely makes sense: it's a powerfully distinct place to be. She fights by - I kid you not - twirling a wooden broomstick around, and by casting spells, though this early on we don't know any.

Instead, combat revolves around one kind of attack, one kind of defense, a dodge, and that's it. Even later on, when spells are available - a petrify spell turns enemies to stone, for example, and a levitate spell lifts them for aerial assaults - that limited core underpins and somewhat stymies the experience. The encounters themselves are wave-based, and you're cinematically locked into a multi-stage encounter until it's cleared.

Enemies appear seemingly out of nowhere, until you've either defeated them or raised your intimidation level high enough to scare them off, which is a nice touch. With attacks landing from all angles, it can feel awkward, and there's a general feeling of scruffiness, or jank, to the build I play. But I like how enemies insult you, and there's a sense of occasion about these staged showdowns.

After combat, I experience more of the game's exploration, and at pace: you can fly on your broom across Amsterdam's watery canals, twirling like Darth Sidious to barge boxes and other light obstacles out of the way. There are specific areas for flying - it's not something you can do everywhere - and it's more like hovering above a surface on a prescribed track than soaring through the air. The experience improves as we arrive in Amsterdam proper, landing on a bridge at night amid a city bustling with activity and people.

Noa, now in civilian form - if she were to stay as a witch, a suspicion gauge would fill and authorities would be alerted, such is the mistrust of witches in the era - proceeds towards a house to find an Original she's come to 'collect' from. You are a sort of debt collector for the devil, you see, and Originals are people who made deals with the devil 333 years ago. As with Assassin's Creed, then, there is an element of hunting a target, a pursuit that will later occupy entire chapters, though to begin it's a simple affair.

A key component of city exploration is the cat, your familiar, which you choose at the beginning of the game and who accompanies your travels. Then in 1999, the roles are reversed: Noa is Aaron's cat. There are a few benefits to having a cat familiar.
